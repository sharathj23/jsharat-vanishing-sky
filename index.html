A shared inheritance

This was everyone's sky.

The Milky Way — our own galaxy, seen edge-on — was once so bright it cast shadows. It guided navigation, seeded mythologies, and anchored calendars on every inhabited continent.

Meet your galaxy

That band of light is home.

The Milky Way isn't just in the sky — it is the sky. It's our own galaxy seen edge-on from the inside: a slowly turning disc of 100–400 billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across. Our Sun is just one of them, halfway out on a quiet spiral arm.

Look up at that glow and you're gazing down the length of the galaxy you live in. And every one of the 8 billion people awake tonight is riding the exact same disc of stars — the one thing the whole world truly shares.

🛰️ The good news: the sky never left

Rise above our layer of city glow and the sky is exactly as brilliant as it ever was. These are real photographs from astronauts on the International Space Station — the same Milky Way our ancestors knew, blazing over a glowing Earth. Only our ground-level light hides it.

🌌 Space vs. the city street

Same planet, two skies. From orbit (first two photos), the Milky Way is unmistakable. That last photo shows why we miss it — the amber glow blanketing the land is the very light we pour upward. The universe didn't change; how we light our nights did — which means we can choose differently.

A new kind of light

And now — the sky has company.

Even the darkest refuges are changing. More than 8,000 active satellites now circle the Earth — with tens of thousands more planned. On a clear night you can watch trains of them slide silently through the stars, streaking astronomers' photos and, more and more, catching the naked eye.

For 200,000 years the night belonged to the stars alone. Now we're learning to share it — and to ask how much light is too much.

Satellite counts approximate, mid-2020s (operator filings / CelesTrak).

The good news

One of the few kinds of pollution we can actually undo.

Here's the honest part: it isn't a single switch. It takes many small choices — and one lost habit. City glow, glowing screens, indoor light, and simply forgetting to look up all play a part. So the fix is both collective and personal. Even so, it's already working: 200+ certified Dark Sky Places are bringing the Milky Way back to millions.

🌙 What the night used to be

When I was small, my mother would feed me dinner out under the open sky. She'd point up at the stars and the big bright moon, and somehow the food went down easier with the whole cosmos watching over us. The night wasn't something to fear. It was the ceiling of our home.

On warm nights whole families would carry out mats and sleep beneath the open sky, and we children would lie flat on our backs and count the stars until our voices gave out — never reaching the end. We'd ask the oldest questions there are: Where do they come from? How are they made? Who lit them, and how far away are they?

I didn't know it then, but I was looking at the same sky my great-grandparents saw, and theirs before them — the one river of light that has connected every human who ever lived. I want my children to be able to look up and feel that too.

Across every century — the living and the long-gone, kings and cave-dwellers, strangers who never shared a language — one single thing has connected every human who ever drew breath.

The Sky.

The same stars. The same moon. The same quiet wonder. It is our oldest, most universal inheritance — and the one we are quietly turning off.

✨ The World After Dark

A short story

The nursing home room smelled of rubbing alcohol and artificial lilies. Leo sat by his grandson Liam's side, listening to the aggressive, rhythmic hum of the city outside the window.

At twenty-four, Liam lived his life in hyper-speed. His thumb flicked across a glowing smartphone screen, illuminating his face in a pale, electric blue. He was physically there, but mentally miles away.

Leo, whose eighty-eight-year-old body was failing him, reached out a trembling hand and gently touched Liam's wrist. "Turn it off for a second, kiddo," Leo whispered, his voice like dry leaves.

Liam blinked, pulling away from the digital world with a faint sigh. "What's up, Grandpa? Do you need the nurse?"

"No," Leo smiled faintly, pointing a frail finger toward the window. "I need you to look out there. What do you see?"

Liam glanced out at the horizon — a dense grid of skyscrapers, neon billboards, blinding streetlights, the yellow headlights of a thousand crawling cars. "I see the city, Grandpa. The lights."

"Exactly," Leo said, his eyes growing distant. "You see the lights we built to chase away the night. But do you know what we locked outside?"

When I was your age, Leo began, my father took me deep into the countryside, far past where the power lines ended. It was pitch black. I was terrified. But he told me to look up — and when I did, I stopped breathing.

"I didn't see an empty black sky, Liam. I saw a river. A glowing, silver, majestic river of a billion stars pouring across the heavens. The Milky Way. So bright it cast soft shadows on the grass beneath our feet."

His father had put an arm around his shoulder and said something Leo never forgot: "For 200,000 years, every single human who ever lived looked up and saw this exact same river of stars. Cavemen, pharaohs, explorers, and your own ancestors. It is the one thing that connects every person who has ever walked this Earth."

Leo looked back at the glowing amber fog of the city. A single tear tracked down the wrinkles of his cheek. "But most people alive today never will."

Liam stared at his grandfather. He looked down at the tiny glowing screen in his hand — the device that kept him connected to everyone online, yet entirely isolated from the universe. Slowly, intentionally, he pressed the power button. The screen went dark.

He placed the phone face down, reached out, and wrapped his young, strong hands around his grandfather's frail, cold one. "Where is that place, Grandpa? The place your dad took you?"

Leo's face broke into a peaceful, radiant smile. "About three hours north. Past the old pine ridge."

Liam squeezed his hand. "As soon as you feel a little stronger, we're going. You and me. We'll go find the river."

For the first time in months, the electronic noise of the world faded into the background. Connected only by the touch of their hands and a shared longing for the ancient dark, the gap between two generations vanished entirely.

🤯 Did You Know?

⏳

Light From the Past

Starlight takes years to reach us — from ~4 years for the nearest star to thousands for distant ones. When you look up, you're literally seeing the past.

🔭

The Farthest You Can See

From a truly dark sky you can spot the Andromeda Galaxy with your bare eyes — 2.5 million light-years away, the most distant thing a human can see unaided.

🌀

A Galactic Year

The Sun takes about 230 million years to circle the galaxy once. The last time we were in this spot, dinosaurs were just beginning to walk the Earth.

🏙️

The 1994 Blackout

After an earthquake blacked out Los Angeles, some residents reportedly called observatories about a strange "silvery cloud" overhead. It was the Milky Way — many had simply never seen it.

🔵

The LED Paradox

Switching to cheap, bright-white LEDs saved energy — but their blue-rich glare scatters more in the air, so many cities actually got brighter, not darker.

🐢

Not Just Us

Artificial light at night disorients sea-turtle hatchlings, migrating birds and fireflies — disrupting species that evolved under naturally dark skies.

Sources: Falchi et al. 2016 (Science Advances) · Kyba et al. 2023 (Science) · NASA · DarkSky International.

✍️ The Data Story

by Sharath · narrated by Drasa

I wanted to visualize something everyone shares but almost no one notices they're losing. The night sky is the one view every human who ever lived has held in common for 200,000 years — and in a single generation, artificial light has quietly erased it for a third of humanity.

This isn't a story told through a static chart. As you scroll, the real star-field behind the words dims in real time, exactly as it has over our cities. Drasa, an AI guide, walks you through the loss so the data feels less like statistics and more like standing outside, looking up. The goal is a single moment of "I had no idea" — followed by the hopeful twist that this is the one kind of pollution that disappears the instant we choose to switch off a light.

— Sharath, Analyticon 2026 VizCon
